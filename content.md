## An oxidation–reduction sequence

Many of the most useful transformations in organic chemistry simply move a molecule up or down an **oxidation ladder**. For a carbon bearing oxygen, a secondary alcohol ($\ce{R2CH-OH}$) and a ketone ($\ce{R2C=O}$) sit one rung apart: take two hydrogens away and the alcohol becomes a ketone (**oxidation**); add them back and the ketone becomes an alcohol (**reduction**). The rigid bicyclic skeleton of the terpene **camphor** makes it a perfect stage for watching both directions.

This lab is the **oxidation half** of a multi-week sequence. Here, the secondary alcohol **borneol** is oxidized up to the ketone **camphor**. In the complementary lab, camphor is reduced back down to the alcohols **isoborneol** and borneol — so the two experiments run the same ladder in opposite directions.

The oxidant is **sodium hypochlorite** ($\ce{NaOCl}$) — ordinary household bleach — activated by **acetic acid**. This is a deliberately **green** choice: it does the same job as classic chromium(VI) reagents without the toxic, carcinogenic heavy-metal waste.

## 2 · Mechanism &amp; pathway

Bleach alone is a sluggish oxidant. Adding **acetic acid** converts hypochlorite into the reactive species, **hypochlorous acid** ($\ce{HOCl}$), which carries out the oxidation. The accepted picture routes the reaction through an **alkyl hypochlorite** (an O–Cl ester) that then eliminates to the ketone.

<div data-mount="pathway"></div>

<div class="mech-grid">
<article class="mech-step"><span class="step-num">1</span>
<h3>Make the active oxidant</h3>
<p>Acetic acid protonates hypochlorite, generating <strong>hypochlorous acid</strong> in situ: $\ce{NaOCl + CH3CO2H -> HOCl + CH3CO2Na}$. $\ce{HOCl}$ carries an electrophilic, "$\ce{Cl+}$-like" chlorine — that is the species that reacts.</p>
</article>
<article class="mech-step major-accent"><span class="step-num">2</span>
<h3>Form the alkyl hypochlorite</h3>
<p>The <strong>oxygen of borneol's –OH</strong> attacks that electrophilic chlorine, displacing water and tying the oxygen to chlorine. The result is an <strong>alkyl hypochlorite</strong>, a C–O–Cl intermediate ($\ce{R2CH-O-Cl}$) that primes C2 for oxidation.</p>
</article>
<article class="mech-step minor-accent"><span class="step-num">3</span>
<h3>E2-like elimination → ketone</h3>
<p>A base removes the <strong>C2–H</strong> while the <strong>O–Cl</strong> bond breaks, chloride ($\ce{Cl-}$) leaving. Those two departures, anti and concerted like an <strong>E2</strong>, forge the C=O double bond of <strong>camphor</strong>. Net: borneol loses $\ce{H2}$ (as effectively $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ + the O–H hydrogen) and is oxidized to the ketone.</p>
</article>
</div>

<div class="callout info">
<h3>Part of a bigger sequence: oxidation ⇄ reduction</h3>
<p>This experiment is <strong>Week 1</strong> of an oxidation–reduction series on the camphor skeleton: <strong>borneol → camphor → isoborneol</strong>. Here we climb the ladder, oxidizing the secondary alcohol <strong>borneol</strong> to the ketone <strong>camphor</strong>. The complementary step runs it back down — reducing camphor with $\ce{NaBH4}$ to <strong>isoborneol</strong> (and borneol) — and is a separate lab: <a href="https://orgolabs.github.io/camphor-reduction/">The Reduction of Camphor</a>. Taken together, the two labs let you oxidize and reduce the <em>same</em> carbon and compare the products.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout">
<h3>Why bleach? A "greener" oxidation</h3>
<p>The traditional way to turn a secondary alcohol into a ketone used <strong>chromium(VI)</strong> reagents (such as $\ce{Na2Cr2O7}$ or the Jones reagent, $\ce{CrO3}$/$\ce{H2SO4}$). They work well but are <strong>toxic, corrosive and carcinogenic</strong>, and leave heavy-metal chromium waste that is expensive to dispose of. <strong>Sodium hypochlorite</strong> does the same oxidation with a cheap, everyday reagent, and its by-products are mostly salt and water — a textbook example of <strong>green chemistry</strong> replacing a hazardous classic.</p>
</div>

The handout uses **racemic** borneol; the 3D viewers use the defined-stereochemistry (1R,2S,4R) record so the geometry renders correctly. The active oxidant is made from **sodium hypochlorite** ($\ce{NaOCl}$, 74.44 g/mol, CID 23665760) and **acetic acid** ($\ce{CH3CO2H}$, 60.05 g/mol, CID 176), which together give hypochlorous acid, $\ce{HOCl}$. ## 3 · Procedure

This is the micro-scale **Week 1** procedure from the bundled course handout. Always follow your instructor's version and your lab's safety rules — this is a study aid, not a substitute for a supervised SOP.

| Reagent | Amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Racemic borneol | 0.360 g | substrate (the 2° alcohol) |
| Acetone | 1.0 mL (+0.5 mL if needed) | solvent (dissolves the borneol) |
| Glacial acetic acid | 0.30 mL | activates the oxidant ($\to \ce{HOCl}$) |
| Sodium hypochlorite solution | 6.0 mL | oxidant (bleach, in excess) |
| *tert*-Butyl methyl ether (MTBE) | 2 mL × 2 | extraction solvent |
| Saturated $\ce{NaHCO3}$ | 2 mL | wash (removes acid) |
| 5% sodium bisulfite | 2 mL | wash (destroys excess oxidant) |
| Water | 2 mL | wash |
| Anhydrous $\ce{Na2SO4}$ | spatula tip | drying agent |

### Week 1 — the reaction

1. Weigh **0.360 g racemic borneol** into a **10 mL round-bottom flask**.
2. Add **1.0 mL acetone**.
3. Add **0.30 mL glacial acetic acid**.
4. Add a **magnetic stir bar**.
5. Fit an **air condenser**.
6. Set the flask in a **water bath at 45 °C**, and keep the temperature in the **40–50 °C** window throughout *(important — too hot and the bleach decomposes and acetone/MTBE flash off)*.
7. **Stir until the borneol fully dissolves** (add **0.5 mL more acetone** if needed).
8. Add **6.0 mL sodium hypochlorite solution dropwise over ~35 min**, down through the air condenser.
9. After the addition is complete, **stir for 15 min more**.

### Work-up (isolating camphor)

10. **Cool to room temperature** and remove the air condenser.
11. Transfer the mixture to a **separatory funnel** and extract the camphor with **2.0 mL MTBE** (shake, venting often).
12. **Separate the layers**, then extract the aqueous layer **again with 2.0 mL MTBE**; **combine** the two MTBE layers.
13. Wash the combined MTBE successively with **2.0 mL saturated $\ce{NaHCO3}$**, then **2.0 mL 5% sodium bisulfite** (this destroys any excess oxidant), then **2.0 mL water**, removing the lower aqueous layer each time.
14. **Dry** the MTBE over **granular anhydrous $\ce{Na2SO4}$**.
15. **Filter** the dried solution into a **pre-weighed vial**.
16. **Evaporate** the MTBE to leave camphor as a white solid.

### Week 2 — analysis

17. **Weigh** the product and calculate the **yield** and **percent yield** (calculator below).
18. Determine the **melting point** — pure racemic camphor melts at **174 °C**.
19. Record the **IR** and **NMR** spectra — look for loss of the broad O–H band and the appearance of a strong ketone $\ce{C=O}$ stretch (~1740 cm⁻¹).

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety:</strong> <strong>sodium hypochlorite</strong> (bleach) is corrosive and oxidizing — never mix it with acid in an uncontrolled way (risk of toxic $\ce{Cl2}$ gas); here the acetic acid is added to the substrate, and bleach is metered in slowly. <strong>Glacial acetic acid</strong> is corrosive with pungent vapor — use a fume hood. <strong>Acetone and MTBE are highly flammable</strong> — no flames or ignition sources. Keep the water bath at <strong>40–50 °C</strong>. Wear goggles and gloves. <strong>Waste:</strong> set aside the aqueous layer per the hood's instructions; organic waste goes to the organic-solvents container; dispose of everything per your lab's procedures.</div>
